## 1. Introduction

The Constitution of India is the supreme legal document governing the relationship between the State and individuals and defining the structure of governmental institutions.

A written Constitution must provide mechanisms through which it can adapt to changing social, political and economic circumstances. Article 368 provides the constitutional procedure for amendment.

However, the existence of an amendment power raises an important constitutional question: whether Parliament can amend every part of the Constitution without limitation.

The Doctrine of Basic Structure emerged as the judicial answer to this question.

The Doctrine does not prevent constitutional amendments. Instead, it establishes that certain foundational characteristics of the Constitution cannot be destroyed through the exercise of the amendment power.

## 2. Historical Background

The constitutional debate concerning amendment power began soon after the Constitution came into force.

The early cases primarily concerned land reform legislation and the protection of property rights.

In *Shankari Prasad v. Union of India*, the Supreme Court upheld Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights.

The same approach was substantially reaffirmed in *Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan*.

However, constitutional disagreement continued regarding the extent of Parliament's power.

In *I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab*, the Supreme Court adopted a restrictive approach and held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights in a manner that abridged them.

The constitutional debate ultimately culminated in *Kesavananda Bharati*.

## 3. Article 368 and the Amending Power

Article 368 provides the constitutional framework for amendment.

The amendment procedure allows the Constitution to evolve in response to changing circumstances.

However, the amendment power itself is a constitutional power.

Parliament does not derive unlimited authority from Article 368.

The Basic Structure Doctrine therefore distinguishes between the power to amend the Constitution and the power to destroy its essential identity.

## 4. Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala

The judgment in *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* was delivered in 1973 by a thirteen-judge Bench of the Supreme Court.

The case involved challenges to constitutional amendments affecting property rights and the relationship between Parliament and constitutional limitations.

By a narrow majority, the Court held that Parliament possessed extensive power to amend the Constitution.

However, Parliament could not alter the basic structure of the Constitution.

The judgment therefore created a balance between constitutional flexibility and constitutional continuity.

## 5. Elements of the Basic Structure

The Supreme Court has not provided an exhaustive list of basic-structure features.

Several principles have nevertheless been recognised through subsequent decisions.

These include:

- Supremacy of the Constitution
- Republican and democratic form of government
- Secular character of the Constitution
- Federal character of the Constitution
- Separation of powers
- Judicial review
- Rule of law
- Free and fair elections
- Dignity and liberty of the individual
- Parliamentary democracy
- Limited amending power

The list remains open and depends upon the constitutional issue presented before the Court.

## 6. Constitutional Supremacy

Constitutional supremacy is an important feature of the Indian constitutional system.

Parliament derives its powers from the Constitution and therefore cannot exercise authority contrary to constitutional limitations.

The Basic Structure Doctrine reinforces this principle by ensuring that the Constitution remains superior to ordinary political majorities.

## 7. Judicial Review

Judicial review enables constitutional courts to examine legislative and executive action.

The Supreme Court has treated judicial review as an important feature of the constitutional structure.

In *Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India*, the Court emphasised the importance of limited governmental power and judicial review.

The Doctrine therefore protects the institutional role of constitutional courts.

## 8. Separation of Powers

The Constitution distributes governmental functions among the legislature, executive and judiciary.

Although India does not follow an absolute separation of powers, institutional independence remains an important constitutional principle.

The Basic Structure Doctrine protects the constitutional balance between these institutions.

## 9. Fundamental Rights

Fundamental Rights constitute a central part of constitutional protection.

The Basic Structure Doctrine does not make every constitutional provision immune from amendment.

However, amendments that destroy the essential constitutional relationship between individual liberty and governmental power may attract basic-structure scrutiny.

The Doctrine therefore plays an important role in protecting the constitutional character of Fundamental Rights.

## 10. Minerva Mills

In *Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India*, the Supreme Court examined constitutional amendments that attempted to expand Parliament's amending power.

The Court held that limited amending power itself forms part of the basic structure.

Parliament cannot use Article 368 to remove the limitations imposed upon its own constitutional authority.

This principle strengthened the Doctrine significantly.

## 11. I.R. Coelho and the Ninth Schedule

The Supreme Court later considered the relationship between the Ninth Schedule and the Basic Structure Doctrine.

In *I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu*, the Court held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 could be examined against the Basic Structure Doctrine if they violated Fundamental Rights forming part of the basic structure.

The decision demonstrated that constitutional protection could not be used to completely exclude judicial review.

## 12. Importance of the Doctrine

The Basic Structure Doctrine serves several constitutional purposes.

First, it protects constitutional identity.

Second, it prevents temporary political majorities from fundamentally altering the constitutional system.

Third, it preserves judicial review and institutional checks.

Fourth, it maintains a balance between constitutional stability and constitutional change.

The Doctrine therefore operates as a safeguard against constitutional authoritarianism.

## 13. Criticism of the Doctrine

The Doctrine has also been criticised.

One criticism is that the Constitution does not expressly use the term "basic structure".

Another criticism concerns judicial subjectivity.

Since the Supreme Court determines which features constitute the basic structure, critics argue that judges receive considerable constitutional authority.

Supporters, however, contend that constitutional interpretation necessarily requires courts to protect foundational principles.

## 14. Constitutional Balance

The Doctrine attempts to balance two competing constitutional objectives.

The first is constitutional adaptability.

The second is constitutional continuity.

An amendment mechanism without limitations could permit the destruction of constitutional democracy.

Conversely, an entirely rigid Constitution could become incapable of responding to social change.

The Basic Structure Doctrine seeks to maintain a middle path.

## 15. Contemporary Relevance

The Doctrine remains relevant whenever constitutional amendments affect democratic institutions, judicial review, Fundamental Rights or the distribution of governmental power.

Modern constitutional disputes increasingly involve technology, privacy, electoral governance and institutional accountability.

The Doctrine provides a constitutional framework within which such developments can be examined.

## 16. Conclusion

The Basic Structure Doctrine is one of the most important contributions of Indian constitutional jurisprudence.

It recognises Parliament's authority to amend the Constitution while simultaneously establishing that the Constitution cannot be fundamentally destroyed through amendment.

The Doctrine protects constitutional supremacy, democratic governance, judicial review and other foundational principles.

Its continuing significance lies in maintaining a balance between constitutional change and constitutional identity.
