## 1. Case Overview

*Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* is one of the most significant constitutional decisions in the history of the Supreme Court of India.

The case concerned the extent of Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368. The petition challenged constitutional amendments and certain State legislation affecting property and related rights.

The judgment is particularly important because the Supreme Court formulated the Basic Structure Doctrine. According to this doctrine, Parliament has a wide power to amend the Constitution, but that power does not extend to destroying or altering the basic structure of the Constitution.

The decision therefore represents a major development in the relationship between constitutional amendment, parliamentary power and judicial review.

## 2. Background of the Case

Kesavananda Bharati was the head of the Edneer Mutt in Kerala.

The dispute arose in the context of land reform legislation enacted by the State of Kerala. The legislation affected the management and property interests associated with the religious institution.

Kesavananda Bharati challenged the relevant legal provisions before the Supreme Court and raised broader constitutional questions concerning Fundamental Rights and the power of Parliament to amend the Constitution.

During the proceedings, the constitutional validity of several amendments became an important issue.

## 3. Constitutional Questions

The case raised fundamental questions concerning the scope of Parliament's constitutional amending power.

Among the important questions were:

1. Whether Parliament's power under Article 368 was unlimited.

2. Whether Parliament could amend or alter Fundamental Rights.

3. Whether a constitutional amendment could be challenged for violating Fundamental Rights.

4. Whether there were any implied limitations on Parliament's power to amend the Constitution.

5. Whether Parliament could alter the essential character or identity of the Constitution.

These questions required the Supreme Court to examine the relationship between constitutional supremacy, parliamentary authority and judicial review.

## 4. Earlier Constitutional Position

Before *Kesavananda Bharati*, the Supreme Court had considered the scope of Parliament's amending power in several important cases.

In *Shankari Prasad v. Union of India*, the Court upheld Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights.

The position was reconsidered in *Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan*.

Later, in *I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab*, the Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights in a manner that would take them outside the protection of Article 13.

These decisions created considerable constitutional debate concerning the extent of Parliament's power under Article 368.

## 5. Constitutional Amendments Involved

The case involved challenges connected with several constitutional amendments, including the Twenty-fourth, Twenty-fifth and Twenty-ninth Amendments.

The Twenty-fourth Amendment was particularly significant because it expressly affirmed Parliament's power to amend the Constitution and clarified aspects of the procedure under Article 368.

The Twenty-fifth Amendment introduced provisions concerning the implementation of certain Directive Principles and limited the scope of judicial review in specified circumstances.

The Twenty-ninth Amendment placed certain Kerala land reform laws in the Ninth Schedule.

The Supreme Court was therefore required to consider whether constitutional amendments themselves were subject to substantive constitutional limitations.

## 6. Judgment of the Supreme Court

The judgment was delivered by a thirteen-judge Constitution Bench, the largest bench constituted by the Supreme Court of India at that time.

By a narrow majority of 7:6, the Court held that Parliament possesses broad power to amend the Constitution.

However, the power to amend the Constitution does not include the power to destroy or alter its basic structure.

This principle became known as the **Basic Structure Doctrine**.

The Court therefore rejected both an unrestricted view of Parliament's amending power and an approach that would completely prevent constitutional amendments affecting Fundamental Rights.

## 7. The Basic Structure Doctrine

The Basic Structure Doctrine is the central principle established by the judgment.

The doctrine means that while Parliament may amend constitutional provisions, it cannot use the amending power to destroy the fundamental identity or essential features of the Constitution.

The Constitution therefore contains certain foundational principles that cannot be removed through the ordinary amendment process.

The doctrine operates as a constitutional limitation on the amending power.

## 8. Elements of the Basic Structure

The Supreme Court did not provide a single exhaustive list of all elements forming part of the basic structure.

However, constitutional features identified through subsequent judicial decisions have included principles such as:

- Supremacy of the Constitution
- Republican and democratic form of government
- Secular character of the Constitution
- Federal character of the Constitution
- Separation of powers
- Rule of law
- Judicial review
- Independence of the judiciary
- Dignity and freedom of the individual
- Unity and integrity of the nation

The precise scope of the doctrine has continued to develop through later constitutional cases.

## 9. Parliament's Power to Amend the Constitution

The judgment recognised that the Constitution must be capable of adapting to changing circumstances.

Parliament therefore retains significant authority to amend constitutional provisions.

However, constitutional amendment is different from constitutional destruction.

The amending power cannot be used to remove the fundamental principles that give the Constitution its identity.

This balance between constitutional flexibility and constitutional continuity lies at the heart of the judgment.

## 10. Judicial Review

The Basic Structure Doctrine strengthened the constitutional role of judicial review.

Under the doctrine, courts may examine whether a constitutional amendment damages or destroys the basic structure of the Constitution.

This ensures that the constitutional amendment process remains subject to constitutional limitations.

Judicial review therefore functions as an important mechanism for preserving constitutional supremacy.

## 11. Fundamental Rights and Constitutional Amendments

The case also addressed the relationship between Parliament's amending power and Fundamental Rights.

The Court recognised that Fundamental Rights are not completely immune from constitutional amendment.

At the same time, the amendment power cannot be exercised in a manner that destroys the basic structure of the Constitution.

The judgment therefore adopted a balanced approach between constitutional amendment and constitutional protection.

## 12. Constitutional Supremacy

The decision reinforced the principle of constitutional supremacy.

In a constitutional democracy, Parliament exercises legislative and constitutional powers within the framework established by the Constitution.

The Constitution therefore remains the highest legal authority.

The Basic Structure Doctrine ensures that the constitutional framework cannot be fundamentally dismantled through the amendment procedure.

## 13. Importance of the Judgment

The judgment has had a lasting influence on Indian constitutional law.

It established a constitutional limitation on Parliament's amending power while preserving Parliament's ability to adapt the Constitution through amendments.

The doctrine has subsequently been applied in several major constitutional cases.

It has also become one of the most distinctive features of Indian constitutional jurisprudence.

## 14. Subsequent Development

The Basic Structure Doctrine has been considered and applied in several later decisions of the Supreme Court.

Cases concerning judicial review, constitutional amendments, separation of powers and judicial independence have relied upon the principles developed in *Kesavananda Bharati*.

The doctrine has therefore evolved beyond the facts of the original dispute and has become an important constitutional safeguard.

## 15. Relationship with Judicial Independence

Judicial independence has subsequently been recognised as an important component of the constitutional structure.

The doctrine has been used to examine constitutional amendments and institutional arrangements that may affect the independence of constitutional courts.

This reflects the broader principle that constitutional institutions must retain sufficient independence to protect constitutional supremacy and the rule of law.

## 16. Constitutional Balance

The judgment represents an attempt to maintain a balance between two important constitutional principles.

On one side is the need for constitutional flexibility and democratic governance.

On the other side is the need to preserve the essential identity and foundational principles of the Constitution.

The Basic Structure Doctrine attempts to reconcile these competing considerations.

## 17. Significance for Indian Democracy

The decision is significant for the functioning of Indian democracy because it places constitutional limits on the exercise of governmental power.

Democratic majorities are permitted to make constitutional changes through the prescribed procedure, but they cannot use constitutional amendment to eliminate the foundational principles of constitutional democracy.

The judgment therefore reinforces the idea that constitutional democracy involves both majority rule and constitutional limitations.

## 18. Critical Perspective

The Basic Structure Doctrine has also generated academic and constitutional debate.

Supporters consider the doctrine an essential safeguard against constitutional abuse and excessive concentration of power.

Critics have raised questions concerning the absence of an exhaustive textual definition of "basic structure" and the role of unelected judges in determining constitutional limitations.

Despite these debates, the doctrine remains firmly established in Indian constitutional jurisprudence.

## 19. Continuing Relevance

The principles established in *Kesavananda Bharati* continue to remain relevant whenever constitutional amendments affect fundamental institutional arrangements or foundational constitutional principles.

The doctrine provides a framework through which courts can examine whether constitutional change remains an amendment or crosses the constitutional boundary into destruction of the Constitution's basic identity.

Its continuing relevance demonstrates the enduring importance of constitutional supremacy and judicial review.

## 20. Conclusion

*Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* remains one of the most important constitutional judgments delivered by the Supreme Court of India.

The decision established the Basic Structure Doctrine and clarified that Parliament's power to amend the Constitution, although extensive, is not unlimited.

The judgment created an important constitutional balance between the need for democratic and constitutional change and the need to preserve the foundational identity of the Constitution.

The Basic Structure Doctrine has subsequently become a central principle of Indian constitutional law and continues to play an important role in protecting constitutional supremacy, judicial review and democratic constitutionalism.
