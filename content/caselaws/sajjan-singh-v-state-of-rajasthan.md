## 1. Introduction

*Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan* is an important decision in the development of Indian constitutional law concerning Parliament's power to amend the Constitution.

The judgment was delivered in the context of challenges to the Constitution (Seventeenth Amendment) Act, 1964.

The case continued the constitutional debate that had begun with *Shankari Prasad v. Union of India* and was later examined more extensively in *I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab* and *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala*.

The principal question before the Supreme Court was whether Parliament possessed the power to amend Fundamental Rights under Article 368 and whether such an amendment could be challenged under Article 13.

The Court, by a majority, upheld the validity of the Seventeenth Amendment and substantially reaffirmed the position adopted in *Shankari Prasad*.

The case is therefore an important stage in the historical development of the law relating to constitutional amendments.

## 2. Case Details

**Case:** *Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan*

**Court:** Supreme Court of India

**Date of Judgment:** 30 October 1964

**Citation:** 1965 1 SCR 933; AIR 1965 SC 845

**Subject:** Constitutional Amendment, Fundamental Rights, Right to Property and Land Reform

**Constitutional Provisions:** Articles 13, 31A, 31B and 368

**Constitutional Amendment:** Constitution (Seventeenth Amendment) Act, 1964

The judgment was delivered by a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court.

## 3. Constitutional Background

The Constitution of India originally protected the right to property as a Fundamental Right.

Articles 19(1)(f) and 31 provided important constitutional protections relating to property.

At the same time, the government was implementing extensive land reform measures.

Several land reform laws were challenged before constitutional courts on the ground that they violated Fundamental Rights.

Parliament responded through constitutional amendments designed to provide greater protection to land reform legislation.

The constitutional validity of these amendments eventually became the subject of a series of landmark Supreme Court decisions.

## 4. The Constitution (Seventeenth Amendment) Act, 1964

The Seventeenth Amendment was enacted against the background of continuing litigation concerning land reform.

The amendment expanded the constitutional protection available to certain categories of land reform legislation.

It also added a substantial number of laws to the Ninth Schedule.

The purpose was to protect those laws from being invalidated on the basis of certain Fundamental Rights.

The validity of the amendment was challenged before the Supreme Court.

## 5. Facts of the Case

The petitioners challenged the constitutional validity of the Seventeenth Amendment.

The amendment had expanded the protection given to certain land reform laws under Article 31A and the Ninth Schedule.

The petitioners argued that Parliament did not possess an unlimited power to amend Fundamental Rights.

They relied upon Article 13 and contended that a constitutional amendment affecting Fundamental Rights should be subject to the limitations contained in that provision.

The dispute therefore required the Supreme Court to reconsider the relationship between Articles 13 and 368.

## 6. Issues Before the Court

The major constitutional issues included:

1. Whether Parliament possessed the power to amend Fundamental Rights under Article 368.

2. Whether a constitutional amendment constituted "law" within the meaning of Article 13.

3. Whether the Seventeenth Amendment was constitutionally valid.

4. Whether the amendment required ratification by the State Legislatures.

5. Whether Parliament's power to amend the Constitution could extend to Fundamental Rights.

6. Whether the decision in *Shankari Prasad* correctly interpreted the relationship between Articles 13 and 368.

## 7. Petitioners' Arguments

The petitioners challenged the amendment on constitutional grounds.

They argued that Fundamental Rights occupied a special position within the Constitution.

According to the petitioners, Parliament could not use the amendment procedure to destroy or substantially alter Fundamental Rights.

They further argued that Article 13 should be interpreted broadly enough to include constitutional amendments.

If constitutional amendments were treated as "law", Parliament would be prevented from making amendments that abridged Fundamental Rights.

The petitioners also questioned whether the constitutional amendment procedure had been properly followed.

## 8. Government's Arguments

The government defended the validity of the Seventeenth Amendment.

It relied upon the reasoning adopted in *Shankari Prasad*.

According to the government, Parliament exercised constituent power when amending the Constitution.

This power was different from ordinary legislative power.

Therefore, a constitutional amendment could not be treated as ordinary "law" under Article 13(2).

The government maintained that Article 368 empowered Parliament to amend every part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, subject to the constitutional procedure.

## 9. Judgment of the Supreme Court

The Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of the Seventeenth Amendment.

The majority substantially reaffirmed the principle laid down in *Shankari Prasad*.

The Court held that the power of constitutional amendment under Article 368 was distinct from ordinary legislative power.

A constitutional amendment was therefore not treated as "law" within the meaning of Article 13(2).

The Court consequently rejected the argument that Article 13 prevented Parliament from amending Fundamental Rights.

## 10. Majority View

The majority view supported the constitutional validity of the amendment.

The judges relied substantially upon the reasoning adopted in *Shankari Prasad*.

The Court considered that Parliament possessed constituent power when acting under Article 368.

Accordingly, the restrictions applicable to ordinary legislation under Article 13 did not automatically apply to constitutional amendments.

The majority therefore upheld the amendment.

## 11. Article 13 and Article 368

The relationship between Articles 13 and 368 was central to the case.

Article 13 protects Fundamental Rights against laws that take them away or abridge them.

Article 368 provides the constitutional mechanism for amendment.

The Court continued to distinguish between ordinary laws and constitutional amendments.

The judgment therefore reaffirmed the position that Article 13(2) did not prevent Parliament from amending Fundamental Rights through Article 368.

## 12. Constitutional Amendment and Constituent Power

The judgment maintained the distinction between legislative and constituent power.

When Parliament passes an ordinary statute, it acts through legislative power.

When Parliament follows the special constitutional procedure for amendment, it exercises constituent power.

This distinction had been recognised in *Shankari Prasad*.

The Court in *Sajjan Singh* substantially accepted that approach.

## 13. Right to Property

The dispute arose during a period when the right to property was protected as a Fundamental Right.

Land reform legislation frequently affected existing property interests.

The government therefore sought constitutional mechanisms to protect reform legislation.

The Seventeenth Amendment was one such constitutional measure.

The case demonstrates the tension between property rights and the State's objective of implementing socio-economic reforms.

## 14. Land Reform and the Ninth Schedule

The Ninth Schedule was introduced by the First Amendment in 1951.

Its purpose was to provide constitutional protection to specified legislation.

The Seventeenth Amendment added further laws to the Schedule.

The constitutional validity of this expansion was challenged in the present case.

The Supreme Court ultimately upheld the amendment.

## 15. The Question of Ratification

Another issue concerned whether the Seventeenth Amendment required ratification by the State Legislatures.

The petitioners argued that the amendment affected constitutional provisions relating to the powers of the courts and therefore required ratification.

The majority did not accept this contention.

The amendment was therefore upheld without requiring the additional ratification argued for by the petitioners.

## 16. Significance of the Judgment

*Sajjan Singh* is significant because it reaffirmed Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights.

The decision strengthened the constitutional position established in *Shankari Prasad*.

It also demonstrated that the Supreme Court was not prepared at that stage to treat Article 13 as a limitation upon the constitutional amendment power.

However, the judgment also revealed growing judicial concern about the extent of Parliament's amending power.

This concern became increasingly important in later constitutional cases.

## 17. Justice Hidayatullah's Concerns

Justice Hidayatullah expressed significant reservations about the breadth of Parliament's amending power.

He questioned whether Fundamental Rights could be treated in exactly the same manner as other constitutional provisions.

His reasoning reflected an emerging concern that unrestricted amendment power could potentially weaken constitutional guarantees.

These concerns became particularly important in the later development of constitutional jurisprudence.

## 18. Justice Mudholkar's Observations

Justice Mudholkar also raised important questions concerning the nature of constitutional amendment.

His observations included consideration of whether the Constitution possessed certain fundamental features that could not simply be altered through the amendment procedure.

Although the Basic Structure Doctrine was not formally established in *Sajjan Singh*, these observations are often viewed as part of the intellectual development that eventually led to that doctrine.

## 19. Connection with Shankari Prasad

*Sajjan Singh* followed the constitutional approach adopted in *Shankari Prasad v. Union of India*.

In *Shankari Prasad*, the Supreme Court had held that a constitutional amendment under Article 368 was not "law" within Article 13(2).

*Sajjan Singh* substantially reaffirmed that position.

The two decisions therefore represent the early judicial approach favouring a broad interpretation of Parliament's amending power.

## 20. Connection with Golaknath

The constitutional position changed significantly only a few years later in *I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab*.

In *Golaknath*, the Supreme Court reconsidered the earlier decisions and adopted a more restrictive approach toward Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights.

The Court held that Parliament could not use the amendment procedure to abridge Fundamental Rights in the manner previously understood.

The shift from *Sajjan Singh* to *Golaknath* represents a major development in Indian constitutional law.

## 21. Connection with Kesavananda Bharati

The constitutional debate eventually culminated in *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala*.

The Supreme Court in *Kesavananda Bharati* recognised that Parliament possessed a wide power to amend the Constitution.

However, the Court held that this power was subject to an important limitation.

Parliament could not alter the basic structure of the Constitution.

The Basic Structure Doctrine consequently became the principal constitutional limitation upon the amending power.

## 22. Evolution of the Constitutional Position

The development of the law may broadly be understood through the following cases:

**Shankari Prasad (1951)** — Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights upheld.

**Sajjan Singh (1965)** — Earlier position substantially reaffirmed.

**Golaknath (1967)** — Parliament's power to abridge Fundamental Rights restricted.

**Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** — Parliament's amendment power recognised but subjected to the Basic Structure Doctrine.

**Minerva Mills (1980)** — Limited amending power and judicial review recognised as important constitutional principles.

This progression demonstrates the evolving nature of constitutional interpretation in India.

## 23. Constitutional Supremacy

The case also contributes to the broader principle of constitutional supremacy.

Parliament derives its authority from the Constitution.

At the same time, Article 368 provides Parliament with a constitutional mechanism to alter the text of the Constitution.

The central question in later cases became whether the amendment power itself had constitutional limits.

This issue ultimately led to the development of the Basic Structure Doctrine.

## 24. Judicial Review

The case is also important in understanding the role of judicial review.

The Supreme Court examined the validity of a constitutional amendment itself.

This demonstrated that constitutional amendments could become the subject of judicial scrutiny.

Although the Court upheld the Seventeenth Amendment, the very exercise of judicial review over the amendment reflected the important constitutional role of the Supreme Court.

## 25. Critical Analysis

From a historical perspective, *Sajjan Singh* reflected the constitutional environment of the 1950s and 1960s.

The government faced practical difficulties in implementing land reforms while complying with Fundamental Rights.

The majority's approach provided Parliament with considerable flexibility to pursue constitutional and socio-economic objectives.

However, the reservations expressed by some judges demonstrated that the question of unlimited amendment power was not fully settled.

These concerns later became central to the constitutional reasoning in *Golaknath* and *Kesavananda Bharati*.

## 26. Importance for Constitutional Law Students

The case is particularly important for students studying the evolution of Parliament's amending power.

It helps explain why the Supreme Court initially accepted a broad interpretation of Article 368.

It also demonstrates how judicial disagreement can influence the future development of constitutional doctrine.

Understanding *Sajjan Singh* makes it easier to understand the later decisions in *Golaknath* and *Kesavananda Bharati*.

## 27. Contemporary Relevance

Although the constitutional position has developed substantially since 1965, the case continues to have historical and doctrinal significance.

Modern constitutional disputes involving amendments, Fundamental Rights and the Basic Structure Doctrine cannot be fully understood without examining the earlier decisions.

*Sajjan Singh* therefore remains an important part of the constitutional history of India.

## 28. Conclusion

*Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan* represents an important stage in the evolution of Indian constitutional law.

The Supreme Court upheld the Seventeenth Amendment and substantially reaffirmed the principle that Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights under Article 368.

The Court maintained the distinction between constitutional amendments and ordinary legislation under Article 13.

At the same time, the separate opinions revealed important concerns regarding the possibility of unrestricted constitutional amendment.

These concerns later contributed to the development of the jurisprudence that culminated in the Basic Structure Doctrine.

The case therefore occupies an important position between *Shankari Prasad* and the later landmark decisions in *Golaknath* and *Kesavananda Bharati*.
