## 1. Introduction

*Shankari Prasad v. Union of India* is one of the earliest and most significant decisions of the Supreme Court of India concerning Parliament's power to amend the Constitution.

The case arose in the context of the Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951, which introduced Articles 31A and 31B and placed certain land reform laws within the Ninth Schedule.

The constitutional validity of the First Amendment was challenged on the ground that it affected Fundamental Rights, particularly the right to property.

The central constitutional question was whether Parliament, while exercising its power under Article 368, could amend provisions relating to Fundamental Rights and whether such an amendment would be subject to Article 13(2).

The Supreme Court upheld the validity of the First Amendment and adopted an important distinction between ordinary legislative power and constituent power.

The judgment subsequently became an important starting point in the evolution of Indian constitutional law concerning Parliament's amending power.

## 2. Case Details

**Case:** *Shankari Prasad Singh Deo v. Union of India and State of Bihar*

**Court:** Supreme Court of India

**Date of Judgment:** 5 October 1951

**Citation:** AIR 1951 SC 458; 1952 SCR 89

**Jurisdiction:** Original Jurisdiction under Article 32

**Constitutional Provisions:** Articles 13(2), 31A, 31B, 32, 368, 379 and 392

**Subject:** Constitutional Amendment, Fundamental Rights, Right to Property and Land Reform

The judgment was delivered by a five-judge Constitution Bench. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

## 3. Constitutional Background

The Constitution of India came into force on 26 January 1950.

During the early years of the Republic, the government introduced several legislative measures aimed at social and economic reform.

One of the most important objectives was the abolition of zamindari and other intermediary landholding systems.

However, several land reform laws were challenged before courts on the ground that they violated Fundamental Rights.

At that time, the right to property was protected as a Fundamental Right, principally through Articles 19(1)(f) and 31.

The constitutional challenges created difficulties for the implementation of land reform programmes.

The Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951 was enacted against this background.

## 4. Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951

The First Amendment made several important changes to the Constitution.

Among other provisions, it inserted Articles 31A and 31B.

Article 31A was designed to provide protection to certain laws relating to agrarian and other specified reforms.

Article 31B provided protection to laws included in the Ninth Schedule from certain challenges based on Fundamental Rights.

The validity of these constitutional changes was challenged in *Shankari Prasad*.

The Supreme Court therefore had to examine whether Parliament possessed the constitutional authority to make such amendments.

## 5. Facts of the Case

The petitioners challenged the constitutional validity of the Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951.

The challenge was connected with legislation concerning land reform and the abolition of zamindari.

The petitioners contended that the First Amendment affected Fundamental Rights guaranteed under Part III of the Constitution.

They particularly relied upon Article 13(2), which restricts the State from making laws that take away or abridge Fundamental Rights.

The petitioners argued that a constitutional amendment affecting Fundamental Rights should also fall within the scope of Article 13(2).

The Supreme Court was therefore required to determine the constitutional relationship between Articles 13 and 368.

## 6. Issues Before the Supreme Court

The principal constitutional questions included:

1. Whether Parliament had the power to amend the Constitution under Article 368.

2. Whether the power under Article 368 extended to the amendment of Fundamental Rights.

3. Whether a constitutional amendment made under Article 368 constituted "law" within the meaning of Article 13(2).

4. Whether Articles 31A and 31B inserted by the First Amendment were constitutionally valid.

5. Whether the First Amendment required ratification by the State Legislatures under the proviso to Article 368.

6. Whether the provisional Parliament was competent to exercise the constitutional amending power.

## 7. Petitioners' Arguments

The petitioners challenged the First Amendment on several constitutional grounds.

One of the principal arguments was that Article 13(2) prohibited the State from making any law that abridged Fundamental Rights.

According to the petitioners, a constitutional amendment affecting Fundamental Rights should therefore be subject to the limitation contained in Article 13(2).

It was also argued that Article 368 prescribed a specific procedure for constitutional amendment and that the provisional Parliament could not exercise the full amending power in the manner in which the First Amendment had been enacted.

Another argument concerned Articles 31A and 31B.

The petitioners contended that these provisions affected the jurisdiction of constitutional courts and therefore required ratification under the relevant provisions of Article 368.

## 8. Government's Position

The government's position was that constitutional amendment under Article 368 involved the exercise of constituent power rather than ordinary legislative power.

Therefore, a constitutional amendment could not be treated in the same manner as an ordinary law made by Parliament.

The government argued that the word "law" in Article 13(2) referred to ordinary legislative enactments and not to constitutional amendments made under the special procedure of Article 368.

Accordingly, Parliament could amend the Constitution even if the amendment affected Fundamental Rights.

## 9. Judgment of the Supreme Court

The Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of the First Amendment.

The Court held that Parliament possessed the power to amend the Constitution under Article 368.

It further held that a constitutional amendment made under Article 368 was not "law" within the meaning of Article 13(2).

Therefore, Article 13(2) did not operate as a limitation upon Parliament's constitutional amending power in the manner argued by the petitioners.

The Court consequently upheld the validity of Articles 31A and 31B introduced by the First Amendment. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

## 10. Article 13 and Constitutional Amendments

Article 13 was at the centre of the dispute.

Article 13(2) provides that the State shall not make any law that takes away or abridges the rights conferred by Part III.

The Supreme Court distinguished between ordinary legislation and constitutional amendments.

According to the Court, ordinary legislation is made through the exercise of legislative power, whereas a constitutional amendment under Article 368 is made through constituent power.

The Court therefore concluded that the expression "law" in Article 13(2) did not include a constitutional amendment made under Article 368. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

## 11. Article 368 and the Amending Power

The judgment gave a broad interpretation to Parliament's power under Article 368.

The Court examined the structure and language of Article 368 and concluded that the provision authorised constitutional amendment through the prescribed procedure.

The Court did not find an implied exclusion of Fundamental Rights from the scope of the amending power.

This interpretation became an important foundation for the early constitutional understanding of Parliament's amending power.

## 12. Distinction Between Legislative and Constituent Power

One of the most important aspects of the judgment was the distinction between legislative power and constituent power.

Ordinary legislation is enacted through Parliament's legislative authority.

A constitutional amendment, on the other hand, is made through the special constitutional procedure prescribed for amendment.

The Court treated these as distinct constitutional functions.

This distinction later became a central issue in cases concerning the extent and limitations of Parliament's power to amend the Constitution.

## 13. Validity of Articles 31A and 31B

The Court upheld Articles 31A and 31B.

These provisions had been introduced to protect certain categories of legislation, particularly land reform legislation, from constitutional challenges based on Fundamental Rights.

The Court rejected the argument that their introduction was beyond Parliament's constitutional authority.

The decision therefore enabled the constitutional protection created by the First Amendment to operate.

## 14. Article 31A and Land Reform

Article 31A was closely connected with the government's land reform programme.

The purpose of the provision was to provide constitutional protection to certain categories of laws concerning acquisition and modification of estates and related matters.

Land reform was considered an important part of the post-independence social and economic programme.

The constitutional protection provided through Article 31A helped prevent certain reform measures from being invalidated solely on the basis of specified Fundamental Rights challenges.

## 15. Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule

Article 31B was another major feature of the First Amendment.

It was accompanied by the creation of the Ninth Schedule.

The Ninth Schedule initially contained specified land reform laws.

Article 31B was intended to protect the laws included in the Schedule from certain challenges based on Part III of the Constitution.

The validity and scope of the Ninth Schedule later became subjects of extensive constitutional litigation.

## 16. Judicial Review

The petitioners argued that Articles 31A and 31B affected the powers of constitutional courts.

The Supreme Court rejected the contention that the provisions required ratification merely because they affected the constitutional treatment of certain laws.

The Court clarified that the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts to exercise their constitutional powers was not simply abolished.

Rather, the constitutional amendment changed the extent to which specified laws could be challenged under Part III. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

## 17. Land Reform and Constitutional Rights

The case demonstrates the constitutional tension between individual property rights and social reform.

After independence, land redistribution and abolition of intermediary landholding systems were important policy objectives.

At the same time, property was constitutionally protected as a Fundamental Right.

The resulting litigation forced the Supreme Court to examine how constitutional rights interacted with legislative programmes aimed at social transformation.

*Shankari Prasad* therefore provides an important example of the Constitution operating as both a protector of individual rights and a framework for social reform.

## 18. Ratio Decidendi

The central principle established by the judgment was that a constitutional amendment made under Article 368 was not "law" within the meaning of Article 13(2).

Consequently, the prohibition contained in Article 13(2) did not prevent Parliament from amending Fundamental Rights through the constitutional amendment procedure applicable at that time.

The Court therefore upheld the First Amendment.

This principle became one of the earliest major judicial statements on the relationship between Fundamental Rights and Parliament's constitutional amending power. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

## 19. Significance of the Decision

The decision was significant for several reasons.

First, it upheld the First Amendment.

Second, it recognised Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights under Article 368 as then understood.

Third, it distinguished constitutional amendments from ordinary legislation for the purposes of Article 13(2).

Fourth, it provided constitutional support for land reform legislation.

Finally, it established an important starting point for the later evolution of the law concerning constitutional amendments.

## 20. Relationship with Sajjan Singh

The reasoning in *Shankari Prasad* was later considered in *Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan*.

The Supreme Court again upheld Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights.

However, questions concerning the scope of Parliament's amending power continued to generate constitutional debate.

The issue was subsequently reconsidered in *I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab*.

## 21. Relationship with Golaknath

The decision in *I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab* represented a significant departure from the position adopted in *Shankari Prasad*.

In *Golaknath*, the Supreme Court adopted a restrictive view concerning Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights.

The Court also applied the Doctrine of Prospective Overruling.

The development from *Shankari Prasad* to *Golaknath* demonstrates the changing judicial understanding of constitutional amendment.

## 22. Relationship with Kesavananda Bharati

The constitutional debate eventually reached a major turning point in *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala*.

The Supreme Court recognised Parliament's broad power to amend the Constitution but held that the basic structure of the Constitution could not be destroyed.

This became known as the Basic Structure Doctrine.

The journey from *Shankari Prasad* to *Kesavananda Bharati* therefore represents one of the most important developments in Indian constitutional law.

## 23. Evolution of the Amending Power

The constitutional position concerning amendment power developed through a series of landmark cases.

A simplified progression can be understood as follows:

**Shankari Prasad** — Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights upheld.

**Sajjan Singh** — Earlier position substantially reaffirmed.

**Golaknath** — Parliament's power to abridge Fundamental Rights restricted.

**Kesavananda Bharati** — Parliament's amending power recognised but subjected to the Basic Structure limitation.

This judicial evolution forms a central part of Indian constitutional jurisprudence.

## 24. Continuing Importance of the Case

Although the constitutional position has evolved considerably since 1951, *Shankari Prasad* remains an important precedent for understanding the historical development of the amending power.

It represents the first major constitutional decision concerning the relationship between Article 13 and Article 368.

It also demonstrates how constitutional interpretation responds to conflicts between individual rights, legislative policy and institutional power.

## 25. Critical Analysis

The judgment can be understood in the historical context of the early years of the Indian Republic.

The government faced significant challenges in implementing land reforms while maintaining constitutional commitments to Fundamental Rights.

The Court's interpretation provided Parliament with substantial flexibility to amend the Constitution.

However, subsequent constitutional developments demonstrated that Parliament's amending power could not remain entirely unrestricted.

The Basic Structure Doctrine eventually established a constitutional limitation upon the amending power.

Thus, *Shankari Prasad* should be understood as an important stage in a much longer constitutional dialogue rather than as the final statement on Parliament's amending authority.

## 26. Contemporary Relevance

The principles discussed in *Shankari Prasad* continue to be relevant whenever questions arise concerning constitutional amendments.

Modern constitutional litigation may involve Fundamental Rights, judicial review, constitutional identity and the limits of Parliament's amending power.

The case provides historical context for understanding why the Supreme Court eventually developed doctrines such as the Basic Structure Doctrine.

It also demonstrates the importance of distinguishing constitutional amendment from ordinary legislation.

## 27. Conclusion

*Shankari Prasad v. Union of India* occupies a foundational position in the development of Indian constitutional law.

The Supreme Court upheld the Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951 and held that a constitutional amendment made under Article 368 was not "law" within the meaning of Article 13(2).

The decision therefore recognised Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights under the constitutional framework then prevailing.

The judgment became the starting point for a long line of constitutional cases concerning Parliament's amending power, including *Sajjan Singh*, *Golaknath* and *Kesavananda Bharati*.

Its importance lies not only in the immediate validity of the First Amendment but also in the constitutional debate it initiated concerning the relationship between Parliament, Fundamental Rights and judicial review.
